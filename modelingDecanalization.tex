\begin{abstract}

  Living organisms continuously manage their homeostatic setpoints but sometimes fall into the ruts and grooves of an entrenched or canalized behavioral repertoire. Clinicians employ interventions, from psychoactive drugs to targeted stimulus regimes, to transiently decanalize entrenched behaviors and support more adaptive patterns. We present a computational model of canalization, decanalization, and recanalization in a homeostatic navigation task. Two policies, trained with a homeostatic reinforcement learning (HRL) objective or a one-sided negative-feedback (NF) objective, were each evaluated in 250 intervention and 250 control trials. Following a temporary perturbation of resource impacts, costs, and traversal history, the HRL agent showed lower mean absolute homeostatic deviation than its control (difference $-7.36$, 95\% CI $[-10.59,-4.14]$) and higher total reward. The NF agent also earned higher reward, but its absolute deviation did not reliably change. The model demonstrates how temporary environmental perturbation can improve subsequent regulation in an agent trained to follow a policy.

\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} canalization, decanalization, homeostatic reinforcement learning, foraging, psychedelics, altered states

\section{Introduction}

Organisms are constantly confronted with the trade-off between exploitation, the pursuit of known sources of reward, and exploration, the pursuit of uncertain sources of reward \cite{stephens_foraging_1986}. Although organisms ideally switch between exploration and exploitation at will and at optimal times \cite{pearson_decision_2014}, they often remain in one of those modes for too long or continue to cycle between them in non-optimal ways \cite{barack_neurocomputational_2016, addicott_primer_2017}. This can lead to habituated behavior patterns that are in conflict with their homeostatic needs \cite{bernard_lectures_1974}. From a dynamical systems perspective, organisms get stuck in the wells of fixed attractors, in the ruts and grooves of a ‘canalized’ landscape of behaviors \cite{waddington_canalization_1959, carhart-harris_canalization_2023}. The Bayesian perspective suggests that precise priors reduce the impact of new evidence, leading to repeated suboptimal behaviors and rigid response patterns \cite{carhart-harris_rebus_2019, carhart-harris_canalization_2023}. Work in reinforcement learning \cite{sutton_reinforcement_1998} has demonstrated that the interaction of meta-parameters and environmental factors can lead to reduced exploration and therefore over-exploitation of rewarding resources \cite{huys_mapping_2013}. These resources are discounted in perceived value, resulting in ‘observational overfitting’, where agents stop exploring altogether because they fixate on the wrong observational factors---resources that once were rewarding but are no longer rewarding \cite{song_observational_2019}.

The problem of hypercanalization (over-canalization) has been recognized in psychology, cognitive science \cite{lloyd_understanding_2023}, clinical practice \cite{addicott_primer_2017}, and integrative psychotherapy \cite{schiepek_self-organization_2014, schiepek_integrative_2015, schiepek_psychotherapy_2017}. In integrative psychotherapy, intentional shifts between decanalization and recanalization are central to the therapeutic process because they allow for the integration and reintegration of positive behavioral patterns. These theorists draw on dynamical systems theory, in particular Haken's synergetics \cite{haken_synergetics_1977}, as well as on control theory and cybernetics \cite{wiener_cybernetics_2019}, to model feedback-driven mental pathologies and possible interventions. Related frameworks include homeostatic setpoint management and allostasis \cite{sterling_allostasis_1988, sterling_allostasis_2012}. In homeostatic reinforcement learning \cite{keramati_homeostatic_2014}, complex behaviors emerge and stabilize over time through learning and the temporal discounting it affords. However, the learning process can backfire if the organism becomes observationally overfitted and relies on rigid expectations about possible rewards in its environment.

On canalization accounts, hypercanalized behaviors such as depression, anxiety, PTSD, substance use disorder, and OCD are said to result from overweighted priors \cite{carhart-harris_canalization_2023}, rigid and stubborn response patterns that canalize behavior toward a downward spiral of maladaptive, self-fulfilling, and self-destructive behavior \cite{scheffer_dynamical_2024}. This view has made its way into psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy, where psychedelics have been proposed to provide an entropic burst that destabilizes brain networks, leading to long-term psychological transformation \cite{carhart-harris_entropic_2014, hipolito_pattern_2023, girn_complex_2023}. These authors propose that focused destabilization during the psychotherapeutic process can address many otherwise intractable conditions marked by behavioral canalization.

Psychedelics may also precipitate critical period plasticity similar to plasticity in early development, leading to ``exquisite sensitivity to environmental input'' \cite[Abstract]{lepow_critical_2021}. This mirrors Juliani et al.'s (2024) description of acute shifts to inference, which, when supported by heightened metaplasticity, result in learning and relearning of the environment and actions that can manipulate it. This learning is made even stronger when the environment itself shifts during these critical periods.

For the most part, these ideas have remained metaphorical. Existing conceptualizations are often suggestive, but they rarely model their assumptions directly. Without a quantitative foundation, their explanatory value is limited. To address this gap, we developed a simulation that translates some of these assumptions into observable behavior. The aim is to render earlier conceptualizations into formal structures that can be quantified and compared, thereby sharpening the concepts, demonstrating their applicability to simulated agents, and exposing open questions that become visible only once the informal ideas are turned into a behavioral model.

Our working model of decanalization is inspired by work on the entropic brain \cite{carhart-harris_entropic_2014,carhart-harris_entropic_2018}, canalization, and Hipólito et al.'s (2023) pattern-breaking model. We also draw heavily on Juliani et al.'s “Deep CANALs” (2024), which directly extends the ``CANAL'' model \cite{carhart-harris_canalization_2023} and distinguishes overfitting in inference from loss of plasticity in learning, treating the intersection of overfitting and low plasticity as a source of psychopathology. According to Juliani et al., decanalization acutely alters synaptic weights, sensory observations, and metaplasticity: “[Acute decanalization] is a function of both the current environmental context (made partially available to the model through a sequence of sensory observations) and the state of the system within the synaptic weight landscape. The inferred set of beliefs in a given instance are a function of the location of the system within the neural activity landscape.” In our agent-based model, sensory observations are observations about internal and external states. Synaptic weights correspond to learned associations between states, actions, and rewards. Neural activity corresponds to short-term beliefs represented by the state variables of the solutions. Metaplasticity is represented by factors that alter the learning dynamics of the system.

Although hypercanalization is expressly maladaptive, it is more modestly rooted in behavioral persistence, which can reflect both learning within an organism and changes that its activities produce in the environment. Established routes can offer material advantages: marine snails following existing mucus trails reduce their own mucus production \cite{davies_energy_2007}, while leafcutter ants construct trails by removing obstacles encountered during travel \cite{bochynek_infrastructure_2019}. Such feedback is central to niche construction: organisms modify their environments, changing the conditions they subsequently encounter \cite{laland_niche_2016}. In real-world environments, agents learn against the backdrop of niche construction. We represent one aspect of this through introducing traversal costs that decrease with repeated visits. The advantage of niche construction is efficiency and reliability, but it can also lead to hypercanalization if the organism becomes over-reliant on a few sources of reward and fails to adapt to new surroundings.

In this paper, we test the hypothesis that a homeostatic reinforcement-trained agent that undergoes simulated decanalization will be better able to minimize its average setpoint distance, relative to an agent that undergoes no simulated decanalization.

As a contrast and sanity check, we also examine the same intervention in an agent trained with a one-sided negative-feedback (NF) objective, which rewards reductions in absolute deviation only when the resulting state remains below the setpoint. Intuitively, we can think of HRL as an adaptive heating and air conditioning system that maintains a stable temperature. In contrast, NF functions as a simple heater would; it only activates when the temperature goes below a single fixed setpoint. We predicted that NF agents would not benefit from decanalization, based on the assumption that NF is fundamentally reactive and, in its simplest form, is not well-suited to proactively adjusting to altered environments. This assumption was drawn from Keramati and Gutkin's (2014) work on homeostatic reinforcement learning, which saw simple HRL models demonstrate improved temporal discounting, resulting in more rapid adaptations to altered environmental conditions, relative to NF. 

To model decanalization and recanalization, we employ a variation of homeostatic reinforcement learning (HRL). This approach links movement, planning, and reward-seeking to the minimization of setpoint deviation. 

Our results suggest that structured interventions like this can succeed in breaking an agent out of the ruts and grooves of sub-optimal behavioral patterns, without the need for top-down control. Altered environments can result in spontaneous, short-term bursts of exploration that benefit homeostatic stability and increased reward. In short, short-term chaos helps agents discover patterns that result in longer-term improvements. Canalization and decanalization are not just metaphors; they can be formalized and modeled in a way that allows for the testing of hypotheses about their effects on behavior. And, in this present study, we show that decanalization can improve homeostatic regulation in a simulated agent.

\section{Methods}

We built a simulation sandbox in Unreal Engine to directly explore canalization and decanalization dynamics. Entrenched behavioral patterns---often visualized as gravitational wells---are represented as variable-cost pathways through which an agent traverses a simulated world of homeostatic state-altering resources. Using reinforcement learning, the agent was trained to choose actions that maximize reward under a given reward function. In each training cycle, the agent observes a state, chooses an action, and receives a reward based on that action. We used an ongoing reward for proximity to the homeostatic setpoint as the primary objective (Homeostatic Reinforcement Learning), contrasting it with a one-sided deficit-reduction objective (Negative Feedback).

The agent is modeled as a cube that moves forward automatically at a constant speed. Its internal homeostatic variable, $h_t$ (which can be thought of as food satiation, with setpoint fixed at 0), decreases through passive loss and tile traversal costs, and increases when the agent overlaps cylindrical resource pellets. Repeated traversals are tracked by partitioning the planar environment into tiles with a memory of past visits. Traversal cost decreases with this visit count, making familiar paths less costly. The arena is enclosed by a rectangular boundary. When the agent crosses this boundary, its position is wrapped\footnote{This design choice prevents edge effects that would otherwise require the agent to manage the boundary as an additional environmental factor and potentially introduce edge-induced artifacts into the behavioral data.} to the opposite side, resulting in a toroidal, arcade-style world.

To train the agent, we used proximal policy optimization (PPO), a model-free reinforcement learning method \cite{schulman_proximal_2017}. PPO tends to be relatively stable and effective in complex learning environments. Both reward conditions used PPO.

We implemented the environment in Unreal Engine 5.5 (Epic Games), a 3D computer graphics game engine that performs physics simulation, rendering, and state management. Although Unreal is a 3D tool, the task itself is essentially planar. To implement reinforcement learning, we used Unreal Engine's Learning Agents plugin, which trains policies in Python 3.11.8 using PyTorch 2.4.0. After training, we loaded encoder, decoder, and policy snapshots for evaluation.

\subsection{Model Details}\label{sec:model}

The agent's internal homeostatic variable $h$ increases on resource collection and decreases through tile traversal and passive loss. Time $t$ indexes simulation ticks of 0.05s (20 Hz) in both training and evaluation; at each tick, the policy receives observations and selects one action, which steers the agent left or right. The agent's homeostatic state is updated according to
\begin{equation}\label{eq:h}
  h_{t+1}=h_t+o_t-c_t(\tau)-l_t,
\end{equation}
where $o_t$ is the value of the pellet collected during tick $t$ (0 if none), $c_t(\tau)$ is the traversal cost of the tile currently occupied, and $l_t$ is the passive loss, applied every fifth tick. Most ticks change $h$ only slightly; collecting a pellet produces a large increase.

The policy also receives estimates of potential homeostatic gain and loss along its central raycast, that is, along the path it would follow if it continued straight ahead. If $\mathbf{O}$ and $\mathbf{T}$ denote the objects intersected by that ray, the corresponding short-horizon homeostatic state estimate can be expressed as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:h-hat}
  \hat h_{t+\kappa}=h_t+\sum_{o\in\mathbf{O}}o-\sum_{\tau\in\mathbf{T}}\hat c_t(\tau)-\hat L_{t,\kappa},
\end{equation}
where $\hat c_t(\tau)$ estimates traversal loss and $\hat L_{t,\kappa}$ estimates passive loss over the projected path, and $\kappa$ is the estimated time needed to traverse it. These observations inform steering actions.

Simulated resource pellet instances (\(o\)) are represented as cylindrical objects that occupy the center of a tile and produce a positive effect on \(h\) on overlap with the agent. The agent is represented as a cube with a bounding box that monitors and manages overlap with \(o\) and \(\tau\) objects. The environment has a $50\times50$ tile grid with spacing of 1,000 Unreal units and a pellet-spawn probability of 0.05 per cell. Ordinary pellet values are drawn uniformly from $[5,35]$. The agent's starting position and orientation are randomized, using different random seeds for training and evaluation so that agents are not evaluated on the starting conditions seen during training. Evaluation samples $h_0$ once per trial from $[-15,15]$.

To model environmental canalization, we created a grid of tiles that reduce \(h\) during overlap with the agent; we call this movement tax \emph{traversal cost}. Each tile is tracked individually, with a base impact parameter of 10 and an overlap count \(n\). Outside decanalization, the tile-impact calculation uses \(0.5/(n+1)\) per tick, where \(n\) counts the agent's entries into the tile during the training episode/trial sequence. Traversal cost therefore decreases with visit count, making familiar paths less costly. This mechanism represents the persistence of activity-dependent environmental changes---a kind of rudimentary niche construction, where the environment adapts to the agent's behavior, with diminishing returns to efficiency.

It should be noted that cost to traversal is not equivalent to punishment. If the agent is above the setpoint, traversal loss can bring it closer to zero and increase subsequent HRL reward. More costly tiles can therefore bring greater reward, depending on the current state. It should also be noted that the agent also canalizes its behavior through reinforcement learning. The agent's policy is trained to maximize reward, which is a function of the homeostatic state \(h\). The agent learns to exploit the environment in ways that reduce \(h\) deviation from the setpoint, and this learned behavior interacts with the environment's traversal costs to produce canalized behavior.

At each tick, the policy---a neural network trained with PPO (Section~\ref{sec:ppo})---receives an array of observations as its input layer. These include predicted homeostatic gain and loss along the current path, the agent's current distance from the setpoint, and seven raycast values that measure the proportion of each ray that intersects with pellets and tiles. The policy network also includes a recurrent layer, which carries 64 internal values from one tick to the next. This acts as a short-term memory, letting the policy use recent experience as well as current observations. The agent's perceptual field spans 90 degrees, using seven raycasts at $-45^\circ$, $-22.5^\circ$, $-11.7^\circ$, $0^\circ$, $11.7^\circ$, $22.5^\circ$, and $45^\circ$ relative to its heading. The agent's perception is limited to 10,000 Unreal units\footnote{An ``Unreal unit'' is the basic unit of measurement in the Unreal Engine but does not translate to real-world distances.} in front of it.

The central 0° raycast is also used for predicting potential homeostatic impact. The agent perceives both potential gain and loss to \(h\) through the central raycast's  overlap with \(o\) and \(\tau\) objects. These estimates correspond to the gain and loss terms of Equation~\ref{eq:h-hat}; the policy receives them separately, alongside $h_t$. The agent uses this information to help make action decisions.

Total loss due to traversal is gathered after each action as the total loss from tiles that intersected with the agent's raycast, assuming \(n+1\) (i.e., that traversal count would increase upon agent overlap).

At each tick, the agent moves forward automatically and selects a single continuous steering command $a_t$, which turns it by $2.5a_t$ degrees.

The agent is unaware of anything beyond these observations and the actions it takes over time (a floating-point steering command at each tick), so its learning is contingent on finding useful interactions between the action (\(a\)) it takes at time \(t\) and the resulting future state at \(\mathbf{S}_{t+1}|a_t\).

\subsection{Homeostatic Reinforcement Learning (HRL)}\label{sec:hrl}

Homeostatic Reinforcement Learning (HRL) models an agent that manages a set of internal physiological or cognitive variables (e.g., energy, temperature, hunger, curiosity). The agent's goal is to reduce drive $D$ by keeping these values close to a set of preferred setpoints \cite{keramati_homeostatic_2014}:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:model-drive}
  D\left(H_t\right)=\sqrt[m]{\sum_{i=1}^N\left|h_i^*-h_{i, t}\right|^n}
\end{equation}
\(H^*=\left(h_1^*, h_2^*, \ldots, h_N^*\right)\) is a vector of setpoints describing the organism's ideal physiological state, and \(H_t=\left(h_{1, t}, h_{2, t}, \ldots, h_{N, t}\right)\) represents the organism's actual physiological state at time \(t\). Note that for the case of $m = n = 1$, which we use, $D$ is a sum of absolute values between the setpoints and the actual physiological states. We focus on the case of a single state \(h_t\), whose setpoint \(h^*\) is set to \(0\). Thus, in our model, drive reduces to the absolute value of $h$: \(D(h_t)=|h_t|\). A setpoint of 0 makes it easy to interpret $h$ values as either above or below the desired setpoint. If $h$ is food-satiation, then positive values are degrees of over-satiation and negative values are degrees of hunger. HRL rewards states near the setpoint, so the agent learns, in Goldilocks fashion, to make $h$ larger when it is below \(h^*\) and smaller when it is above \(h^*\).

Keramati and Gutkin (2014) define reward as reduction in drive, $r_t=D(H_t)-D(H_{t+1})$, and show that an agent maximizing discounted reward of this kind also minimizes its discounted deviation from the setpoint. We reward closeness to the setpoint directly. At each tick, the HRL agent receives a reward that is largest at the setpoint and shrinks as $h$ moves away from it in either direction:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward-hrl}
  r_t^{HRL}=\frac{\Delta t\,R}{1+(h_t/S)^2},\qquad \Delta t=0.05,\ R=1,\ S=25.
\end{equation}

In effect, the organism is being rewarded for minimizing drive over time. Reward falls to half its maximum at $|h_t|=S$ and is never negative except on failure (below). For example, each tick spent at the setpoint earns 0.05, a tick at $h=\pm25$ earns 0.025, and a tick at $\pm50$ earns 0.01. An agent that stayed at the setpoint for a whole phase (2,000 ticks) would earn 100; one that stayed 25 away would earn 50. $R$ and $S$ are scaling parameters that set the reward magnitude and the width of the reward curve. They were selected to improve agent performance, measured as mean distance from the setpoint during pre-trial analysis for agent snapshot selection. The multiplier $\Delta t$ scales each tick's reward by its duration. Training episodes normally last 100s, but end early if $h$ gets more than 100 away from the setpoint ($|h_t|>100$). The agent then receives a reward of $-1$, the only negative reward, and is reset for a new episode. Evaluation trials never end early: each runs the full 300s (three 100s phases), however far $h$ drifts.

\subsection{Homeostatic Negative Feedback (NF)}\label{sec:nf}

We also trained agents with a negative feedback (NF) reward function, which rewards the agent only for moving closer to the setpoint $h^*$ when it is below it. A heater thermostat is similar in that it only acts on one side of the setpoint: the heater turns on when the temperature gets too low, but no action is taken when the temperature gets too high.

Formally,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward-nf}
  r_t^{NF}=\begin{cases}
    \max\bigl(|h_{t-1}|-|h_t|,0\bigr)\,R/S,& h_t<0,\\
    0,& h_t\geq0,
  \end{cases}
  \qquad R=1,\ S=25.
\end{equation}
The reward is proportional to how much closer to the setpoint the agent moves, but only if it ends the tick below the setpoint. For example, going from $h=-20$ to $-18$ earns $2/S=0.08$, while going from $-20$ to $-22$ earns nothing; there is no penalty. A pellet that takes $h$ from $-30$ to $-5$ earns 1, but one that takes it from $-10$ to $+15$ earns nothing, since the agent ends above the setpoint. $R$ and $S$ are scaling parameters, as in HRL. As with HRL, a training episode ends early if $h$ gets more than 100 away from the setpoint, but here the final reward is zero.

We chose NF as a contrast to HRL. Keramati and Gutkin (2014) argued that HRL, unlike classical negative-feedback accounts, explains anticipatory responding, learned cue-triggered responses that reduce deviations from a temperature setpoint. We therefore predicted that decanalization would have little or no effect on NF agents. NF and HRL share the property of rewarding reduced deviation from the setpoint, but NF only rewards movement toward the setpoint when below it, resulting in behavior that is more reactive than proactive and therefore unstable.

\subsection{Learning via Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)}\label{sec:ppo}

Neural-network policies were trained with proximal policy optimization \cite{schulman_proximal_2017} through Unreal Engine's Learning Agents interface and project-local Python adapters. HRL and NF use different hyperparameters, and NF required fine-tuning to effectively learn. These are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation-settings}.

Training used a fixed timestep of 0.05s (20 Hz). Episodes terminate if $|h_t|>100$ and truncate when the episode timer exceeds 100s. Full resets restore $h_0=-15$, clear recurrent memory and custom reward/termination bookkeeping, reset perception and tile history, restore resources and their respawn timers, and clear stale movement input.

HRL and NF selection prioritized encoder/policy/decoder snapshot triplets that received the highest mean reward, chosen from pre-trial analyses of agent performance.

\subsection{Canalization and Decanalization}\label{sec:protocol}

We represent canalization both through policy training and through the environmental history left by repeated traversal. Once training ends, the policy's weights remain fixed, but its behavior still depends on observations, recurrent state, and the paths made less costly by earlier visits. This simulation translates ideas about canalization in inference and learning \cite{juliani_deep_2024} into environmental alterations.

We represent decanalization through temporary changes to the environment and to what the agent perceives. During decanalization, every pellet is worth 35 (the maximum ordinary value), and traversal and passive losses are set to zero. These changes affect the agent’s estimates of future homeostatic gain and loss.

The environmental changes include halving tile traversal counts after decanalization and removing the counting of tile traversals during decanalization\footnote{The removal of tile traversal counting during decanalization was sensitivity tested---it did not significantly affect performance, nor did increasing traversal counting do the same. The only result of increasing tile traversal was an increase in deviation from the ideal setpoint, but not enough to meaningfully affect results. HRL with decanalization still saw significantly improved performance versus HRL without decanalization.}. 

These alterations are designed to simulate the effects of decanalizing stimuli being actively explored in psychedelic research. %TODO: Mini lit review and explanation of why these alterations are appropriate for simulating decanalization.

Each trained agent is evaluated in 250 canalization--decanalization--recanalization (CDR) trials and 250 canalization--canalization--canalization (CCC) trials. Each phase comprises 2,000 ticks of 0.05s, or 100s; phase boundaries occur at 2000, 4000, and 6000 cumulative ticks.

In the first phase in both conditions, the agent is evaluated in a canalized environment with ordinary resource and cost settings. 

In the second phase, CDR applies decanalization, while CCC retains ordinary resource and cost settings, with homeostatic value reset to the initial value for that trial between phases.

In the third phase, CDR restores ordinary resource and cost settings but halves (rounds down) the visit counts to represent the flattening of the behavioral landscape, returning the agent to a canalizing perceptual and environmental state. CCC retains ordinary resource and cost settings.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis and Reproducibility}\label{sec:stats}

All 250 trials in each condition were logged. The primary analysis reports CDR minus CCC mean differences, two-sided Welch tests, and unadjusted 95\% confidence intervals. We also ran paired comparisons, matching CDR and CCC trials by trial number. No formal a priori power analysis was performed.

\subsection{Paired comparisons}

Because CDR and CCC trials share the same random seeds, each CDR trial can be paired with the CCC trial of the same number. Before the intervention, the paired trials produced identical outcomes in all 250 pairs for both agents. The recorded starting scenes differed slightly in a few pairs, however (for example, in a pellet's respawn timer; 20 HRL and 24 NF pairs). We therefore repeated the analysis as paired comparisons, both across all pairs and restricted to pairs whose scenes matched exactly. The results were essentially unchanged: decanalization reduced the HRL agent's mean absolute $h$ by 7.4 across all pairs and 7.3 across exact matches, and increased the NF agent's reward by 2.9 and 3.0, with no reliable change in its mean absolute $h$.

\section{Discussion}\label{model-discussion}

% In this paper, we test the hypothesis that a homeostatic reinforcement-trained agent that undergoes simulated decanalization will be better able to minimize its average setpoint distance, relative to an agent that undergoes no simulated decanalization.

% As a contrast and sanity check, we also examine the same intervention in an agent trained with a one-sided negative-feedback (NF) objective, which rewards reductions in absolute deviation only when the resulting state remains below the setpoint. Intuitively, we can think of HRL as an adaptive heating and air conditioning system that maintains a stable temperature. In contrast, NF functions as a simple heater would; it only activates when the temperature goes below a single fixed setpoint. We predicted that NF agents would not benefit from decanalization, based on the assumption that NF is fundamentally reactive and, in its simplest form, is not well-suited to proactively adjusting to altered environments. This assumption was drawn from Keramati and Gutkin's (2014) work on homeostatic reinforcement learning, which saw simple HRL models demonstrate temporal discounting, resulting in more rapid adaptations to altered environmental conditions.

We hypothesized that decanalization would improve post-intervention homeostatic regulation for the HRL agent. The results support this hypothesis: the HRL agent had lower post-intervention mean absolute deviation under CDR than CCC, maintaining a narrower range around the setpoint. Likewise, we predicted that the NF agent would not benefit from decanalization. This was the case, at least in terms of staying close to the setpoint. However, it was actually able to increase reward despite its inability to maintain homeostatic regulation. This suggests that decanalization can improve performance in some dimensions without improving homeostatic regulation.

Using HRL and NF objectives with PPO, we simulated a canalization--decanalization--recanalization process in which repeated traversal makes familiar pathways less costly. This represents a limited form of niche construction: activity changes the environment, which then shapes subsequent behavior \cite{pio-lopez_scale-free_2025, lyon_reframing_2021}. Efficient routes can support stable behavior while also reducing the incentive to explore alternatives.

The phase 1 behavior illustrates what we mean by hypercanalization. The agent over-exploits a local cluster of resources and stops exploring the larger space. Given what it can perceive, a 90-degree field reaching about ten tiles, the local loop is the best available option: distant pellets are invisible, and leaving the worn path means paying full traversal cost on unfamiliar tiles. In therapeutic terms, the policy behaves as if this is the best it can do. This is a form of the observational overfitting described above \cite{song_observational_2019}. Decanalization temporarily removes the cost of leaving the rut and makes every pellet equally attractive, which lets the agent reach routes and resources it would otherwise not try. The greater $h$ loss, $h$ gain, and pellet count of the HRL agent after decanalization are consistent with this wider ranging, and the 7.4-point reduction in mean absolute $h$ gives one estimate of how much the canalized agent stood to gain.

The NF agent also earned more reward after the intervention, but its regulation did not clearly improve. The distinction could be attributed to the differences in objectives: HRL rewards sustained proximity to zero, whereas NF rewards reductions in absolute deviation while the resulting state remains negative. Repeated deficit reduction can earn NF reward without maintaining a narrow range around zero. Even though decanalization allowed the NF agent to earn more reward (and therefore significantly improve performance in some dimensions), it did not improve its ability to actually maintain homeostatic regulation around the setpoint. Comparing it back to a heater thermostat, merely activating the heater more often does not mean that the temperature will be maintained closer to the setpoint; it may still fluctuate widely above and below it.

Both policies can anticipate through discounted PPO, but NF required more training and fine-tuning. 

Cybernetic accounts of personality place goal regulation at the center of individual differences \cite{safron_deyoung_2021}, and recent work develops this program using active inference \cite{safron_sheikhbahaee_2024}. Our model makes a narrower distinction between stable behavior under ordinary conditions and behavior after a temporary perturbation.

Safron et al. propose that psychedelic effects can involve both strengthened and relaxed beliefs, depending on representational level, dose, and context \cite{safron_albus_2025}.

Temporary perturbation also has a loose parallel with simulated annealing, where destabilization can facilitate movement between regions of a solution space \cite{kirkpatrick_optimization_1983, hajek_cooling_1988}. This contribution is a concrete example of improved subsequent regulation, providing a computational starting point for studying behavioral transformation \cite{clingoDisintegrationReintegrationModelPsychological2025}.

\subsection{Limitations}

The evaluation compared just a single agent per objective. It also did not measure variation across independent training seeds. Cross-agent differences therefore cannot fully isolate the effect of reward design from training settings.

At phase boundaries, $h$ was restored to the trial's initial value while other within-trial state was retained. Post-intervention performance is consequently not uninterrupted physiological recovery. This decision was made intentionally: during decanalization, pellets are worth the maximum and losses are removed, so $h$ can move far from the setpoint, and we did not want that deviation to carry into the recanalization phase. However, this came at the cost of our being unable to measure the effect of decanalization on uninterrupted homeostatic recovery. 

Some initial-scene captures also differed in timer, visibility, or overlap state despite identical baseline outcomes and matching RNG and memory records. The paired comparisons address the recorded differences; the general significance or direction of results do not meaningfully change, even after compensating for these factors.

Homeostatic summaries are based on event/update samples rather than uniformly timed samples. Related outcome measures share information, confidence intervals are unadjusted, and no optimal controller or independent training-seed benchmark was evaluated; the residual gap to attainable performance is therefore unknown.

The HRL policy is not optimal. We used off-the-shelf PPO with limited tuning and no systematic hyperparameter search, and the task is partially observed (a 90-degree field of view reaching roughly ten tiles of a $50\times50$ arena) and non-stationary (tile costs change with visits and pellets respawn). Both training settings and the structure of the environment therefore likely contribute to the gap. Our aim was comparative rather than to maximize performance: in domains such as Atari games and language modeling, finding good working solutions has required very large amounts of compute and expert engineering, even where, as in language modeling, no zero-loss optimum exists \cite{mnih_human-level_2015, badia_agent57_2020, hoffmann_training_2022}.

In the primary runs, visit counts did not increase during decanalization and were halved at recanalization.
We explored sensitivity to changes in visit counts during decanalization but did not find that these significantly impacted the primary results. However, we did not explore sensitivity to other factors, such as the practice of halving visit counts after decanalization. There were many possible parameters that could have been adjusted and explored in detail. The chosen values were selected primarily for learning tractability and to capture some essential alterations present in decanalization as understood by psychedelic researchers and other researchers interested in pivotal behavioral changes, but they may not be optimal or inclusive of all relevant factors. Future work could explore the sensitivity of the results to these parameters. That said, the parameters and hyperparameters were selected independently of the evaluation data, and the main results were robust to the paired comparisons.

Another limitation is that this model explores the management of a single setpoint. Living organisms coordinate around the management of many different setpoints, presenting constant pressure for organisms to effectively juggle these, allowing some to stray far from the ideal range so that others may be satisfied. In such cases, the far-from-ideal setpoint(s) exert disproportionate influence on the organism's behavior (e.g., when we are extremely thirsty, all we can think about is quenching that thirst).

In real-world environments, endlessly exploitable resources, perfect predictions, and environmental constancy are improbable. Moreover, the optimal reward is often unknown, and setpoints---as in allostasis \cite{sterling_allostasis_1988}---are themselves changing over time. Organisms have to deal with many trade-offs and risks. Learning to use drive to motivate discounted behaviors that are well-adapted to long-term survival means being able to live with non-ideal states.

A fuller treatment of between-trial differences, robustness to hyperparameter choices, and ablations of the composite decanalization manipulation would strengthen future work.

An area that we did not address---but could support long-term behavioral shifts---is intentional reintegration driven by top-down processes. Psychedelic-assisted therapy is characterized by acute alterations in experience, but this is followed by active reintegration practices \cite{bathje_psychedelic_2022}. The present intervention changes environmental conditions without a separate reintegration procedure, whereas real-world patients receive support before, during, and after treatment. The effects of behavioral nudging on decanalized and recanalized agents could help to demonstrate the dynamics more clearly.

\subsection{Future Directions}

The present study applies a single decanalization episode to a policy whose weights are fixed after training. A natural next step is to apply repeated cycles of decanalization and recanalization to agents that continue learning, and to ask which schedules lead to recovery and which use the perturbation as fuel for long-term improvement. We expect a long-term Goldilocks zone. With too little decanalization, the agent stays in its rut. With too much, it is constantly destabilized and never has time to consolidate what it finds, so repeated decanalization should eventually degrade performance. Somewhere in between, periodic decanalization followed by enough recanalization to learn from it should produce the best long-term regulation, much as annealing depends on its cooling schedule \cite{kirkpatrick_optimization_1983, hajek_cooling_1988}. The same framework could test robustness to new disruptions, a computational analogue of new adversity or trauma: shifting or depleting the resources the agent relies on, or moving its setpoint, and comparing how quickly previously decanalized and never-decanalized agents recover. More generally, the model supports many such studies, varying the timing, intensity, and frequency of interventions and the conditions that follow them, and measuring their long-term rather than only immediate effects.

These questions bear on a broader debate about optimal dosing and the shape of therapeutic change. One view treats change as incremental: a small intervention is given, its effects observed, and the dose increased or decreased accordingly. This is the standard model in behavioral and cognitive-behavioral therapy, and it may explain the continued appeal of psychedelic microdosing, even though placebo-controlled studies have so far found little benefit beyond placebo \cite{szigeti_microdosing_2021}. A newer view, associated with integrative psychotherapy and synergetics \cite{schiepek_self-organization_2014, schiepek_psychotherapy_2017}, introduces criticality into psychological transformation. Change on this view is not smooth; it comes from one or a few highly transformative episodes, such as pivotal mental states \cite{brouwer_pivotal_2021} or transformative experiences \cite{paul_transformative_2014}, in which a system near instability reorganizes. Discontinuous change appears even within cognitive-behavioral therapy, where some patients show large sudden gains between sessions \cite{tang_sudden_1999, hayes_change_2007}. Our model can compare these possibilities directly, for example by giving agents either one large perturbation or many small ones of matched total magnitude and asking which produces more durable improvements in regulation. The present results, in which a single large intervention improved subsequent regulation, are consistent with the pivotal view but do not test it against an incremental alternative.


\section{Conclusions}

Research on canalization and decanalization often moves between clinical, dynamical, and mechanistic vocabularies without making underlying computational assumptions explicit. Our aim was to formalize one such set of assumptions in a simple model that could be inspected, perturbed, and compared across conditions.

We showed that temporary decanalization improved setpoint regulation for an HRL-trained agent, while an NF-trained agent earned more reward without regulating better. This suggests that decanalization strategies have the potential to improve a person's ability to manage their needs, but the effect is not guaranteed and depends on many contextual factors.

Like most scientific models, ours is a simplified approximation of a much more complex reality. We reduced the number of possible parameters for tractability, and in doing so may have omitted emergent and higher-level features that are central to behavioral transformation. Although the model was simple, the implementation was not. Many technical and parameter choices may have shaped the results. These are standard tradeoffs in scientific modeling and reflect the need to study behavioral entrenchment with a concrete model. The model should therefore be read as a formal demonstration of one candidate mechanism, not as a direct account of psychiatric disorders, psychotherapeutic change, or psychedelic treatment outcomes. We nevertheless hope it provides a useful starting point for further investigation.
