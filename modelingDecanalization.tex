\begin{abstract}

  Living organisms continuously manage their homeostatic setpoints but sometimes fall into the ruts and grooves of an entrenched or canalized behavioral repertoire. Clinicians employ interventions, from psychoactive drugs to targeted stimulus regimes, to transiently decanalize entrenched behaviors and support more adaptive patterns. We test these ideas with a computational model in which canalization is learned: a foraging agent builds a cognitive map and becomes increasingly confident about where food is. Policies were trained with a homeostatic reinforcement learning (HRL) objective or a one-sided negative-feedback (NF) objective in a stable world. We then compared how they coped when the world changed: familiar resources degraded and better ones appeared elsewhere. We tested whether decanalization (temporarily less confident beliefs and more random choices) followed by recanalization improved on continued canalization. Under continued canalization, most HRL agents became trapped, returning to failing resources. Decanalization helped trapped agents find the new resources sooner and regulate better. HRL agents benefited significantly more than NF agents.

\end{abstract}

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} canalization, decanalization, homeostatic reinforcement learning, foraging, psychedelics, altered states

\section{Introduction}

Organisms are constantly confronted with the trade-off between exploitation, the pursuit of known sources of reward, and exploration, the pursuit of uncertain sources of reward \citep{stephens_foraging_1986}. Although organisms ideally switch between exploration and exploitation at will and at optimal times \citep{pearson_decision_2014}, they often remain in one for too long or cycle between them in suboptimal ways \citep{barack_neurocomputational_2016, addicott_primer_2017}. This can lead to habituated behavior patterns that are in conflict with their homeostatic needs \citep{bernard_lectures_1974}. From a dynamical systems perspective, organisms get stuck in the wells of fixed attractors, in the ruts and grooves of a ``canalized'' landscape of behaviors \citep{waddington_canalization_1959, carhart-harris_canalization_2023}. The Bayesian perspective suggests that precise priors reduce the impact of new evidence, leading to repeated suboptimal behaviors and rigid response patterns \citep{carhart-harris_rebus_2019, carhart-harris_canalization_2023}. Reinforcement learning models describe such behavior \citep{sutton_reinforcement_1998}, linking anhedonia and depression to reduced sensitivity to reward, rather than to slower learning \citep{huys_mapping_2013}. Learning agents can also fail in a way that resembles canalization: in ``observational overfitting'', they latch onto features of the observations they were trained on that no longer guide behavior well when those observations change \citep{song_observational_2019}.

The problem of hypercanalization (over-canalization) has been recognized in psychology, cognitive science \citep{lloyd_understanding_2023}, clinical practice \citep{addicott_primer_2017}, and integrative psychotherapy \citep{schiepek_self-organization_2014, schiepek_integrative_2015, schiepek_psychotherapy_2017}. In integrative psychotherapy, intentional shifts between decanalization and recanalization are central to the therapeutic process because they allow for the integration and reintegration of positive behavioral patterns. These theorists draw on dynamical systems theory---including Haken's \citeyearpar{haken_synergetics_1977} synergetics, control theory, and cybernetics \citep{wiener_cybernetics_2019}---to model feedback-driven mental pathologies and possible interventions. Related frameworks include homeostatic setpoint management and allostasis \citep{sterling_allostasis_1988, sterling_allostasis_2012}. Keramati and Gutkin \citeyearpar{keramati_homeostatic_2014} model homeostatic regulation in terms of learning to maximize an objective---drive reduction---in a complex environment, which results in the emergence of sophisticated behaviors, such as temporal discounting. However, the learning process can backfire if the organism becomes observationally overfitted and relies on rigid expectations about possible rewards in this environment.

In canalization accounts, hypercanalized conditions such as depression, anxiety, PTSD, substance use disorder, and OCD are asserted to result from overweighted priors \citep{carhart-harris_canalization_2023} that produce rigid and stubborn response patterns, canalizing behavior toward a downward spiral of maladaptive, self-fulfilling, and self-destructive behavior \citep{scheffer_dynamical_2024}. This view has been applied to psychedelic-assisted psychotherapy, where the acute effects of psychedelics are proposed to increase entropy, destabilizing brain networks and leading to long-term psychological transformation \citep{carhart-harris_entropic_2014, hipolito_pattern_2023, girn_complex_2023}. These authors propose that focused destabilization during the psychotherapeutic process can address many otherwise intractable conditions marked by behavioral canalization.

Psychedelics may also precipitate critical-period plasticity similar to plasticity in early development, leading to ``exquisite sensitivity to environmental input'' \citep[Abstract]{lepow_critical_2021}. This mirrors Juliani et al.'s (2024) description of acute shifts to inference, which, when supported by heightened metaplasticity, result in learning and relearning of the environment and actions that can manipulate it. This learning is made even stronger when the environment itself shifts during these critical periods.

For the most part, these concepts have remained metaphorical. Existing conceptualizations are often suggestive, but they rarely model their assumptions directly. Without a quantitative foundation, their explanatory value is limited. To address this gap, we developed a simulation that translates some of these assumptions into observable behavior. The aim is to render earlier conceptualizations into formal structures that can be quantified and compared, thereby sharpening them, demonstrating their applicability to simulated agents, and exposing open questions that become visible only once these informal concepts are turned into a behavioral model.

Our working model of decanalization is inspired by work on the entropic brain \citep{carhart-harris_entropic_2014,carhart-harris_entropic_2018}, canalization, and Hipólito et al.'s (2023) pattern-breaking model. We also draw heavily from Juliani et al.'s ``Deep CANALs'' (2024), which directly extends the ``CANAL'' model \citep{carhart-harris_canalization_2023} and distinguishes overfitting in inference from loss of plasticity in learning, treating the intersection of overfitting and low plasticity as a source of psychopathology---hypercanalization. Juliani et al. distinguish two optimization landscapes: one over neural activity, which supports inference, and one over synaptic weights, which supports learning. What the system believes at any moment depends on where it sits in the activity landscape. That position is shaped by two things: what the system currently observes, and where it sits in the weight landscape \citep[][``Two Canalizations'' section, para.~7]{juliani_deep_2024}. 

In our agent-based model, sensory observations correspond to the agent's perception of its internal and external state, synaptic weights represent fixed weights in the agent's learned policy, and neural activity represents the agent's beliefs about its world, held in a cognitive map where precision grows with experience. The resulting strong beliefs act as a canalizing, behaviorally restrictive force that persists well after the world the agent learned about has changed. We selectively apply decanalization to weaken those beliefs, which allows new behaviors to emerge.

In our implementation, we start by training policies in a diverse range of stable worlds, rewarding actions based on one of two pre-selected objectives representing the maintenance of a homeostatic setpoint. Next, we place those trained agents in similarly shaped worlds and evaluate their performance as these worlds are altered. In these worlds, the agents initially build confident beliefs about familiar resources and establish routes whose traversal costs decrease with use, even as repeated harvesting depletes those resources. After a fixed time interval, new, reliable resource patches appear, altering the world. For a second fixed time interval, agents either undergo temporary decanalization---relaxed belief precision and more variable choices---or receive no intervention. We call the first condition \emph{CDR}, for canalization, decanalization, and recanalization, and the second \emph{CCC}, for canalization throughout. Both conditions then run for a third fixed time interval, in which the decanalized agent returns to its usual settings and its beliefs regain precision. This final phase tests whether changes in beliefs and behavior support subsequent regulation. Learned policy weights remain fixed throughout evaluation. Figure~\ref{fig:overview} sketches this design, and Figure~\ref{fig:movement} follows one simulated pair of agents through these phases.

We test two hypotheses. First, temporary decanalization---relaxing the precision of the agent's beliefs and increasing the variability of its choices---will improve the agent's ability to regulate its homeostatic state after the intervention ends. Second, in the same worlds and with the same intervention, an agent trained with a homeostatic reinforcement learning (HRL) objective will benefit more than an agent trained with a one-sided negative-feedback (NF) objective. 

The contrast between HRL and NF follows \citet{keramati_homeostatic_2014}, who argued that homeostatic reinforcement learning, unlike classical negative-feedback accounts, explains anticipatory regulation and the temporal discounting of future rewards---both central to survival in complex and demanding environments. Intuitively, HRL resembles a heating and air-conditioning system that maintains a stable temperature; NF resembles a heater that acts only when the temperature falls below a setpoint. The one-sided nature of the NF objective tends to limit its ability to promote anticipatory regulation.

\section{Methods}

We built a simulation in which an agent manages a single homeostatic variable while foraging in a two-dimensional world, learns where resources are, and gains confidence in its beliefs as they are confirmed. In this model, canalization corresponds to the precision of the agent's expectations in its cognitive map: having specific expectations about where food is corresponds to being canalized. Someone who has lived in a neighborhood for years has precise expectations about what is around every corner, while a visitor to a new city does not, and a precise belief takes much more contrary evidence to revise \citep{yon_precision_2021}. Decanalization corresponds to a temporary relaxation of that precision, together with an increase in the randomness of the agent's choices, in line with the REBUS account of relaxed beliefs under psychedelics \citep{carhart-harris_rebus_2019} and the distinction between inference and behavioral variability in Deep CANALs \citep{juliani_deep_2024}.

\subsection{World}\label{sec:world}

The world is a $50\times50$ toroidal grid of unit tiles. At each tick of $\Delta t=0.05$\,s, the agent moves forward at 5 tiles per second and turns by up to $10^\circ$. Its homeostatic state $h$ (setpoint 0) changes as
\begin{equation}\label{eq:h}
  h_{t+1}=h_t+o_t-\left(c(n)+\ell\right)\Delta t,
\end{equation}
where $o_t$ is the value of a pellet harvested during the tick, $\ell=2$ is a passive loss per second, and $c(n)$ is the traversal cost per second of the occupied tile, which falls with $n$, the number of times the agent entered the tile before the current crossing. The tile cost at any given point in time is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wear}
  c(n)=c_{\min}+(c_0-c_{\min})\,e^{-n/k},\qquad c_0=5,\ c_{\min}=1,\ k=3,
\end{equation}
where the tile's cost goes down each time the agent enters it. Moving at 5 tiles per second, the agent spends about 0.2\,s on each tile, so a crossing costs about $0.2\,c(n)$. The first crossing of a tile ($n=0$) costs $0.2\times5=1$ unit of $h$. The second ($n=1$) costs about $0.2\times3.9\approx0.8$, and the cost approaches 0.2 with repeated crossings. Entry counts fade with a half-life of 200\,s, so a worn path persists only through use.

Traversal cost models a simple form of niche construction, in which organisms modify their environments and so change the conditions they later encounter \citep{laland_niche_2016}. Established routes can offer material advantages: marine snails that follow existing mucus trails produce less mucus of their own \citep{davies_energy_2007}, and leafcutter ants build trails by clearing obstacles as they travel \citep{bochynek_infrastructure_2019}. In the same way, a path in our world gets easier to travel the more it is used. Worn paths make familiar routes cheaper, which can also make leaving them less attractive.

Resources occur in ten patches of $3\times3$ cells, placed randomly throughout the world, with at least two cells separating patches. Each patch has a pellet value drawn uniformly from $[3,15]$, and each of its cells holds one pellet of that value. Pellets regrow 8 seconds after harvesting in a fully fertile patch. Regrowth takes longer as fertility falls: the delay is $\frac{8}{f}$ seconds, where $f\in(0,1]$ is the patch's fertility. Every harvest multiplies $f$ by $0.92$, so after ten harvests $f$ is about 0.43 and regrowth takes about 18\,s. When a patch is not being harvested, its fertility recovers gradually: the gap between $f$ and 1 shrinks to about 37\% of its size every 300\,s. Thus, a well-harvested patch at $f=0.43$ would recover only to about 0.79 after five minutes.

In each evaluation trial, the agent first forages in the world, and the patches it learns to rely on degrade with use. At 300\,s, two new patches appear, at least 20 tiles from the agent's current position. Their pellet values (uniform $[8,12]$) are in the middle of the range of the original patches. These are designed to be better overall: unlike the original patches, they never degrade, and they regrow a pellet in 4\,s, half the time of a fully fertile original patch. This models a positive change in circumstances, like an animal whose familiar food sources are running thin when better ones appear elsewhere, or a person whose living situation suddenly shifts, presenting new opportunities and challenges. The agent whose familiar patches are failing suddenly has a better option available, outside the area where it habitually forages. We call this environment and its shifts the \emph{changing world}, and all evaluation takes place in instances of it.

Policies are trained in a simpler, stable version of the world, in which harvesting does not lower fertility and no new patches appear, so a familiar route never stops paying. This sets up the question the study asks: when a habit that served well stops paying, does temporarily loosening it help the agent adapt?

\subsection{Agent: perception and cognitive map}\label{sec:map}

The agent perceives along seven rays, spanning $90^\circ$ with a range of 10 tiles. The agent uses these perceptions to maintain a set of beliefs about the pellets it has observed---a simple cognitive map. Beyond its ray perception, the agent also always knows its own position, and the map stores what it believes is at each location, so it can head directly to a remembered cell from anywhere. It does not have to learn the layout itself, since the world is an open field with no walls or obstacles. For each cell $c$, the map holds two numbers: $V_c$, the value of the pellet the agent believes is there (0 if it believes the cell is empty), and $P_c$, the precision of that belief. Precision is the weight a belief carries against new evidence. It grows each time the cell is observed, reflecting how much recent evidence supports the belief. At each tick, the precision of every cell fades, and each cell the agent observes is updated toward the pellet value $v$ it sees there ($v=0$ for an empty cell):
\begin{equation}\label{eq:belief}
\begin{aligned}
  \text{Fading:}&\quad P_c\leftarrow P_c\,e^{-\Delta t/\tau},\\
  \text{Observation:}&\quad V_c\leftarrow\frac{P_cV_c+P_ov}{P_c+P_o},\qquad P_c\leftarrow P_c+P_o,
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
where $\tau=200$\,s is the time constant of fading and $P_o$ is the precision of the observation. $P_o$ depends on the kind of observation. In each tick, every cell the agent sees gives $P_o=0.05$ (1 per second), whether or not it holds a pellet; standing on an empty cell gives $P_o=0.025$ (0.5 per second); and harvesting a pellet gives $P_o=2$. The agent's own cell is often also in view, so standing on an empty cell can add both. The new believed value is an average of the old belief and the observation, each weighted by its precision, and the precisions add. This is a standard Bayesian update for a Gaussian belief, where each observation is independent evidence. Fading means that beliefs the agent does not refresh lose weight, so new evidence moves them more easily. For example, a cell the agent has harvested and watched many times might have a precision of around 50. If the agent then sees it empty for one second, its believed value drops from 10 only to about 9.8 ($50\times 10\div51$). A cell glimpsed for two seconds has a precision of about 2, and the same one-second look drops its believed value from 10 to about 6.7 ($2\times10\div3$). Cells the agent harvests often hold precise beliefs, and later evidence that they are empty is slow to weaken those beliefs.

The agent combines its beliefs about cell values with an expectation that harvested cells will always regrow in 8\,s, as a fully fertile patch does. The believed worth of a cell is therefore $V_c\,(1-e^{-(t-t_c)/8})$, where $t_c$ is the time of its last harvest.

\subsection{Policy}\label{sec:policy}

The agent's behavior is controlled by a learned policy that chooses from among four options. In reinforcement learning, an option is a fixed behavior that, once chosen, runs for a period of time \citep{sutton_between_1999}. Every 0.5\,s, the policy gives a probability for each option, one option is sampled, and its fixed steering rule runs until the next choice. The four options are to \emph{seek} the remembered cell with the greatest believed worth, discounted by distance; \emph{explore} toward the nearest cell never seen; \emph{cruise} along the cheapest (most worn) direction; or \emph{avoid} visible food. The policy learns when to use each option; the direction to travel comes from the cognitive map and the steering rules. The policy's ten inputs are the homeostatic state $h$; the highest pellet value in view and the distance to the nearest visible pellet; the believed worth, distance, and precision of the best remembered cell; the distance to the nearest unexplored cell; the fraction of the world seen so far; the cost of the current tile; and the time since the last harvest. The policy is a two-layer network of 64 units per layer, with separate policy and value outputs.

\subsection{Homeostatic reinforcement learning (HRL)}\label{sec:hrl}

Homeostatic reinforcement learning (HRL) models an agent that manages a set of internal physiological or cognitive variables (e.g., energy, temperature, hunger, curiosity). The agent's goal is to reduce drive $D$ by keeping these values close to a set of preferred setpoints \citep{keramati_homeostatic_2014}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:model-drive}
  D\left(H_t\right)=\sqrt[m]{\sum_{i=1}^N\left|h_i^*-h_{i, t}\right|^p}
\end{equation}
Here, \(H^*=\left(h_1^*, h_2^*, \ldots, h_N^*\right)\) is a vector of setpoints describing the organism's ideal physiological state, and \(H_t=\left(h_{1, t}, h_{2, t}, \ldots, h_{N, t}\right)\) represents the organism's actual physiological state at time \(t\). For $m=p=1$, which we use, $D$ is a sum of absolute differences between the setpoints and the actual physiological states. We focus on a single state \(h_t\), whose setpoint \(h^*\) is set to \(0\), so that $D=|h_t|$. A setpoint of 0 makes it easy to interpret $h$ values as either above or below the desired setpoint. If $h$ is food satiation, then positive values are degrees of over-satiation and negative values are degrees of hunger.

\citet{keramati_homeostatic_2014} define reward as drive reduction, and we use this definition directly. At each tick, the HRL agent earns
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward}
  r^{\mathrm{HRL}}_t=0.2\,(D_{t-1}-D_t)=0.2\,\bigl(|h_{t-1}|-|h_t|\bigr).
\end{equation}
HRL rewards movement toward the setpoint from either side, so an HRL agent learns, in Goldilocks fashion, to raise $h$ when it is below \(h^*\) and to let it fall when it is above \(h^*\). Each unit that $h$ moves toward the setpoint earns a reward of 0.2, and each unit it moves away costs 0.2. The factor 0.2 sets the overall size of rewards for training but does not change what the reward favors. For example, a pellet worth 10 that takes $h$ from $-12$ to $-2$ earns a reward of 2. A pellet that takes $h$ from $-4$ to $+6$ overshoots and costs 0.4, since the agent ends farther from the setpoint than it started. Losses work the same way: below the setpoint, passive and traversal losses cost reward, but above it they move $h$ back toward the setpoint and earn reward.

\subsection{Negative feedback (NF)}\label{sec:nf}

We also trained agents with a negative-feedback (NF) reward function, which counts only deficits. A heater thermostat is similar in that it only acts on one side of the setpoint: the heater turns on when the temperature gets too low, but no action is taken when the temperature gets too high. NF reward depends only on changes in the deficit $\delta_t=\max(-h_t,0)$, which is zero whenever $h$ is at or above the setpoint:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:reward-nf}
  r^{\mathrm{NF}}_t=0.2\,(\delta_{t-1}-\delta_t).
\end{equation}
Below the setpoint, NF works like HRL: each unit that $h$ moves toward the setpoint earns a reward of 0.2, and each unit it moves away costs 0.2. The pellet that takes $h$ from $-12$ to $-2$ again earns a reward of 2, and falling from $-20$ to $-22$ costs 0.4. Above the setpoint, nothing is rewarded or penalized. The pellet that takes $h$ from $-4$ to $+6$ earns 0.8 for removing the deficit, where HRL charged 0.4 for the overshoot. NF agents therefore have no reason to avoid overeating, and a surplus can even help them, since it delays the next deficit.

Following \citet{keramati_homeostatic_2014}, we chose NF as a contrast to HRL. They argued that HRL, unlike classical negative-feedback accounts, explains anticipatory responding, such as learned cue-triggered responses that reduce deviations from a temperature setpoint. NF is a simple version of such an account: like HRL, it rewards the agent for regulating $h$, but only deficits matter to it. Although NF agents can learn to act in advance---partly aided by the learning algorithm we selected---they do not respond as well to decanalization.

\subsection{Training}\label{sec:training}

Policies were trained in stable worlds with proximal policy optimization (PPO) \citep{schulman_proximal_2017}, a standard reinforcement learning method for neural network policies. The network has two outputs, trained together: a policy, which gives a probability for each option, and a value estimate, which predicts the reward still to come and is used only during training, to judge whether a choice turned out better or worse than expected. Each update shifts the policy toward choices that turned out better than expected, but only by a limited amount, which keeps training stable. Two features of this setup directly affect our results. First, the policy is stochastic: the agent samples its choices from the policy's probabilities, in training and in evaluation, which is what allows the temperature boost to make them more random. A small bonus for keeping those probabilities spread out during training stops the policy from becoming fully deterministic. Second, future reward is discounted by 0.99 per tick, so reward 5\,s ahead counts about a third as much as reward now.

Training comprised 60 iterations, each with 16 trials lasting 700\,s (about 1.3 million decisions total), in randomly generated stable worlds. We trained four HRL and four NF policies with different random seeds; each seed determined the initial weights and training worlds. A snapshot was saved after every iteration. For each policy, we evaluated snapshots from every fifth iteration starting at iteration 20, as well as the final snapshot, on eight validation trials in new stable worlds. We kept the snapshot that best met the policy's own objective: lowest mean $|h|$ for HRL or lowest mean deficit for NF. The policies no longer learn after training. Settings are listed in Appendix~\ref{app:implementation-settings}.

\subsection{Canalization, decanalization, and recanalization}\label{sec:protocol}

Each evaluation trial takes place in the changing world and has three phases (Figure~\ref{fig:protocol}). In the canalization phase (0--300\,s), the agent starts at $h=0$ with an empty map, forages, and builds up its beliefs. At 300\,s, the two new patches appear and the intervention phase (300--400\,s) begins. In the recanalization phase (400--700\,s), the agent continues in the changed world with its usual settings. The homeostatic state carries over from one phase to the next, so an agent that falls into deficit in one phase starts the next one hungry. The intervention never changes $h$ directly, so any difference in $h$ between conditions comes from how the agents behave and how that behavior changes the world.

We compare two conditions. In the CCC condition (canalization throughout), the agent receives no intervention. In the CDR condition (canalization, decanalization, recanalization), the agent is decanalized during the intervention phase. Decanalization has two components. Precision relaxation lowers the precision of every belief, so that new evidence revises beliefs more easily, and a temperature boost increases the randomness of the agent's choices. In the main setting, every precision is set to zero at the start of the intervention phase, and the temperature is doubled until the phase ends.

At full strength, beliefs carry no weight against what the agent sees for the whole phase. Each sighting of a cell replaces the belief about it, so a single glimpse of an empty patch is enough for the agent to believe it is empty. Standing on or harvesting from a cell updates the belief as usual, weighed against whatever precision the cell has gained since the phase began. The temperature controls how random the policy's choices are, since the policy chooses among its options by sampling. Raising it from 1 to 2 makes the options the policy would usually pass over more likely. After the intervention phase, the temperature returns to 1, and sightings are again weighed against the precision each belief has built up since the intervention began, as in Equation~\ref{eq:belief}.

Two settings control the strength of the intervention. The dose $d$, between 0 and 1, scales both components: every precision is multiplied by $1-d$ at the start of the phase, and the temperature is raised from 1 to $1+b\,d$ until the phase ends. The temperature boost $b$ sets how far the temperature rises at a given dose. Throughout the phase, whenever the agent sees a cell, the belief's precision is multiplied by $1-d$ again when it is weighed against the sighting, although the stored precision is not lowered further. At half dose, then, a belief formed before the intervention carries a quarter of its usual weight against what the agent sees, and half its usual weight when the agent stands on or harvests from the cell. The main setting, $d=1$ and $b=1$, gives the zero precision and doubled temperature described above. To quantify how the strength of the intervention matters, we tested every combination of a half or full dose ($d=0.5$ or 1) with a boost of 0, 1, or 3, and, at full dose only, a boost of 10. To see what each component contributes, we tested precision relaxation alone (boost 0) and the temperature boost alone (boost 1 or 3, with precision unchanged).

Finally, to check whether precise beliefs are what trap the agent, we ran each policy with beliefs that never become precise and with no intervention. In this low-precision control, harvests add no precision, and precision fades with a time constant of 20\,s instead of 200\,s, so the agent's beliefs mostly reflect what it currently sees.

\subsection{Measures and statistics}\label{sec:stats}

We compare the two conditions on four measures (Table~\ref{tab:measures}). The main measure is how far, on average, $h$ stays from the setpoint after the intervention has ended: mean absolute $h$ in the recanalization phase, which we call MAE (mean absolute error). Agents are trained to regulate $h$, so this is the most direct measure of their performance. Because $h$ is unbounded, a trapped agent can fall far into deficit, and a few such trials can pull mean values well beyond typical ones; medians are given in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:main}. The other three measures help explain differences in MAE. The fraction trapped shows how often agents fail badly, mean $h$ shows whether they err toward deficit or surplus, and new-patch harvests, counted separately during and after the intervention, show whether and when they find the new resources. Except for new-patch harvests during the intervention, all measures are taken in the recanalization phase.

Each of the eight trained policies (four HRL and four NF, one per training seed) was evaluated on 64 trials in each condition. The trials are paired: a CCC trial and its CDR partner share the world layout, starting state, and random events, so they are identical until the intervention begins, and any later difference between them comes from the intervention. To draw conclusions that hold across independently trained policies, and not only across trials of one policy, we treat each policy as a single observation. For each policy, we average the 64 paired differences, and we report the mean across the four policies of each type with a 95\% $t$-interval ($df=3$) and a two-sided $p$ value. To compare HRL with NF, we pair the HRL and NF policies trained with the same seed, which share initial weights, training worlds, and evaluation trials, and test the four differences between their effects in the same way. Model development used separate evaluation seeds.

\section{Results}\label{model-results}

We show that canalization becomes maladaptive in the changing world, test the two hypotheses, and then turn to the exploratory question of how the components and intensity of the intervention affect outcomes. For all results, values are means across the four training seeds; confidence intervals and $p$ values are given in the tables and figures.

The policies were competent in the world they were trained in: on the held-out stable-world trials used to select them, HRL policies kept mean $|h|$ near 9, and NF policies kept their deficit near zero, albeit with large surpluses. With either policy, agents did poorly in the changing world. Their familiar patches began to degrade from the start, and many agents kept returning to them as they failed. Before the new patches appeared, $h$ had already fallen to $-100$ or below in 62\% of HRL trials and 48\% of NF trials (Figure~\ref{fig:performance}). Trapping persisted: in the recanalization phase, the fractions were 70\% for HRL and 48\% for NF (Table~\ref{tab:ccc-cdr}). Many CCC agents found the new patches only well after the intervention phase had ended, by which time most HRL agents were deep in deficit, while many NF agents, once they found them, overate. Others never found them: by the end of the trial, 36\% of HRL and 24\% of NF CCC agents had not harvested from them. Without the new patches, many HRL agents would have kept declining. In exploratory runs of the changing world without them, extended to 1,200\,s, about two thirds of HRL trials ended at $-100$ or below, most of them still falling, while about a quarter ended near the setpoint.

Two things led agents to become trapped: overly precise beliefs and unanticipated changes to the world. Compared with the low-precision control---which keeps precision low for the whole trial---agents with precise beliefs were trapped more often in the recanalization phase, by 24 percentage points for HRL and 33 for NF. HRL agents with precise beliefs also regulated worse, with an MAE 60 points higher; for NF, the difference was smaller and uncertain. When training covered the world change, trapping nearly disappeared: an HRL policy trained in the changing world itself was trapped in just 2\% of trials, even without decanalization, and regulated well (MAE 12.5). For this policy, decanalization had little to improve: it changed MAE by only $-0.7$ at a boost of 3. These comparisons are fully reported in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:main}.

Our second hypothesis was that HRL agents would benefit more than NF agents, and they did. Decanalization helped both, but in different ways. On the main measure, HRL agents gained far more. Decanalization lowered their MAE by 83 points, against 34 for NF agents, and the NF benefit shrank toward zero at higher boosts. The difference held for every pair of HRL and NF policies trained with the same seed. This was not because NF agents failed to escape. Decanalization freed even more NF agents than HRL agents from trapping (33 percentage points fewer trapped, against 23), and NF agents also reached the new patches sooner. The difference came afterward. As the mean $h$ panels of Figure~\ref{fig:performance} show, decanalized NF agents escaped their deficit and then overshot, ending far above the setpoint (mean $h$ of $+112$). They also harvested somewhat more from the new patches than decanalized HRL agents did (33 pellets against 27), consistent with overeating. NF reward ignores everything above the setpoint, so once fed, an NF agent has no reason to stop eating.

Finally, as an exploratory dimension, we investigated what drives the effect, testing each component of the intervention alone and changing its strength (Figure~\ref{fig:levers}). Precision relaxation alone helped much more than raising the temperature to 2, and about as much as raising it to 4. Together they helped most. With a temperature of 2, the combination helped more than the sum of the two components' separate effects. With a temperature of 4, the combination helped most of all, but less than the sum of the separate effects, which suggests that the two components partly work the same way: both let the agent leave patches its beliefs still favor.

We also varied the intensity of the intervention (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:main}). For HRL agents, a larger dose was better: half doses helped less than full doses at every boost tested. For the boost, a middle value was best. At full dose, the benefit rose from 59 points with no boost to 83 at a boost of 1 and 98 at a boost of 3. A boost of 10, tested on three of the four training seeds, helped less than a boost of 3 on the same seeds (72 points against 91) and made regulation during the intervention itself much worse (MAE of 108 against 62). Every setting tested lowered HRL agents' MAE. NF agents showed neither pattern. At full dose, their benefit was 35 points with no boost, no larger with a boost of 1 (34 points), and smaller with boosts of 3 and 10 (28 and 9 points).


\section{Discussion}\label{model-discussion}

We hypothesized that decanalization would improve post-intervention homeostatic regulation in an HRL agent, and that an NF agent would benefit less. Both predictions held. Canalization here is a learned property: an agent that gathers food reliably in a stable world becomes confident about where food is. That confidence is rewarding while the world stays the same. When the world changes, the same confidence is punishing, holding the agent to patches that no longer pay, even as better resources appear elsewhere. This is hypercanalization, in the sense of maladaptive persistence after the conditions that once justified a habit have gone \citep{carhart-harris_canalization_2023}. 

Not all CCC agents stayed trapped. Many eventually found the new patches and, once there, harvested from them about as often as CDR agents did. But they found them late, often after falling deep into deficit, and more than a third of HRL CCC agents never found them within the trial. Decanalized agents reached the new patches sooner and spent far less time far from the setpoint. The clinical parallel is that many people recover from entrenched patterns without intervention; it simply takes them longer, and the cost lies in the time spent far from where they need to be and the consequences of extreme deviations from the ideal.

Both HRL and NF agents escaped their failing patches, but only the HRL agent is rewarded for staying near its setpoint once it does. The NF agent's reward ignores everything above the setpoint, so nothing tells it to stop once it is full. In the terms of our heating analogy, it is a heater that gets credit for warming a cold room but no penalty for overheating it. Once freed, decanalized NF agents kept eating past the setpoint and ended far above it (mean $h$ of $+112$), so their regulation improved much less than that of HRL agents. Decanalization gave both agents a chance to reach better food, and what each did with that chance depended on what it was rewarded for. This is the point of Keramati and Gutkin's \citeyearpar{keramati_homeostatic_2014} homeostatic account. An HRL agent is rewarded for moving toward its setpoint from either side, so for it, seeking reward and staying stable are the same thing. For an NF agent, they can come apart---instability and even large deviations from the setpoint are common.

Decanalization worked on the agent's beliefs, which is where its canalization lived. Lowering its confidence in what it believed about each cell let new evidence change its cognitive map more easily. This relaxing of beliefs is the core of the REBUS account \citep{carhart-harris_rebus_2019}. REBUS concerns high-level priors, such as beliefs about oneself and the world, while ours are low-level beliefs about where food is, so the parallel is one of mechanism rather than content.

Psychedelic effects may involve both relaxed and strengthened beliefs, depending on representational level, dose, and context \citep{safron_albus_2025}; our intervention only relaxes. Raising the temperature of the agent's choices made them more random, letting it sample options its beliefs disfavored. Each component helped on its own, but their combination helped most, consistent with the distinction in Deep CANALs between rigid inference and reduced behavioral variability \citep{juliani_deep_2024}. Neither component changed the agent's physiology, the reward function, or its learned weights; the benefit came from what the agent did with a temporarily loosened view of its world, and persisted after the intervention ended.

Both components have a natural reading in active inference, where each is a reduction in precision: of beliefs, and of policy selection, since sampling temperature is the inverse of policy precision. In that framework, setpoints play the role of prior preferences, and homeostatic regulation is treated as inference about interoceptive states \citep{pezzulo_active_2015}; the same framework has been used to model personality as goal regulation \citep{safron_sheikhbahaee_2024}. We built our agent with standard reinforcement learning but have not formulated it in active inference terms. The framework is a natural companion to this work, but was not our focus here.

For HRL agents, the intensity results form an inverted U: an intermediate temperature boost worked best, while a very high one disrupted regulation during the intervention and helped less afterward. This is the shape predicted by analogies with simulated annealing \citep{kirkpatrick_optimization_1983, hajek_cooling_1988}: destabilization helps when it gets a system out of a suboptimal attractor, but hurts when it destroys useful structure. 


The model also bears on how rigidity figures in psychopathology. Carhart-Harris et al.\ \citeyearpar{carhart-harris_canalization_2023} propose that canalization, an entrenched rigidity of beliefs and behavior, is the general factor of psychopathology. Cybernetic accounts of personality, which place goal regulation at the center of individual differences, read the general factor differently. On this reading, the general factor is associated with high neuroticism and low agreeableness and conscientiousness \citep{caspi_p_2014}, and so corresponds fairly closely to low stability---the Big Five metatrait that protects goals, interpretations, and strategies from disruption \citep{safron_deyoung_2021}. These accounts define psychopathology itself as a persistent failure to move toward one's goals, caused by a failure to generate effective new goals, interpretations, or strategies when existing ones stop working. A model like ours with a single kind of agent cannot settle which dimension is general, since that is a question about how individual humans differ. However, it does show that canalization need not be harmful in itself. The canalized agent regulated well in the stable world it was trained in, and it became trapped only when the world changed in ways training had not covered. And an agent trained in the changing world itself was almost never trapped, suggesting that experience with a particular change can prevent being trapped by this same change. Moreover, a trapped agent fits the cybernetic definition closely: its familiar patches stopped paying, and it did not find a new strategy in time. What harmed the agent was not canalization as such but canalization that no longer fit the agent's world.

Decanalization need not come from outside. \citet{brouwer_pivotal_2021} propose that pivotal mental states---brief periods of heightened plasticity that can arise naturally under stress---help people leave patterns that no longer serve them. The psychedelic experience has been described as following the narrative arc of the Hero's Journey, with a departure, initiation, and return \citep{brouwer_trajectory_2024}. Our three phases have a similar shape. Read this way, our implementation suggests one reason such pivotal states could be adaptive: a brief loosening, coming when the world has changed, shortens the time spent far from the setpoint, avoiding the homeostatic consequences of extreme divergence.

Our approach builds on the long-standing research tradition surrounding cognitive maps. Rats and people get around using learned maps of their world, supported by place and grid cells \citep{okeefe_hippocampus_1978, hafting_microstructure_2005, ekstrom_cellular_2003}. Such maps extend to abstract and social knowledge more broadly \citep{behrens_cognitive_2018}. In a now-classic case, Tolman's maze-navigating rats were compelled to find a new route when a familiar one was blocked \citep{tolman_cognitive_1948}. Our agents faced a related problem when their familiar patches failed, except that their map itself was wrong and had to be revised. Since their maps changed more easily, our decanalized agents found the new food much sooner, avoiding much of the deficit that our hypercanalized agents fell into. Tolman also linked the concept of cognitive maps to personality and human well-being. He contrasted people who have narrow ``strip'' maps with those who have broad, comprehensive ones, and suggested that overly repetitive training and frustrating conditions could narrow a person's way of approaching the world---an early precursor of the canalization concept. Our model shows, in a simple case, that a confident map guides an agent's choices well so long as it aligns with the world. When it no longer fits, an ill-aligned map can imprison the agent, and loosening it can free the agent to seek new opportunities, realigning itself in the process.

Our account also has an embodied counterpart to hypercanalization. Part of what happens to $h$ is set by the world rather than by the agent's beliefs: every step costs energy, and the cost depends on how worn the path is. Agents carve out local niches for themselves, wearing paths that make familiar routes cheaper while depleting the patches they come to rely on---a limited form of niche construction \citep{laland_niche_2016, pio-lopez_scale-free_2025}. This is consistent with embodied views of cognition, on which cognition is rooted in both an organism's physiology and its environment \citep{lyon_reframing_2021}, and with the view that entrenched patterns span brain, body, and world \citep{hipolito_pattern_2023}. Clinically, this is a familiar point. Hypercanalized behaviors are maintained and continuously reinforced by routines, relationships, and settings---not only by beliefs---and interventions that target only internal rigidity may leave external grooves intact.

This work also has a phenomenological interpretation. As a person moves through a world that is only transiently stable, their cognitive map can shape what stands out and what recedes in their field of experience \citep{gurwitsch_field_1964, yoshimi_extending_2015}. As they learn about their environment, their perception changes with it \citep{yoshimi_husserlian_2016}, including what they find salient \citep{clingoDisintegrationReintegrationModelPsychological2025}. In our agent, precise beliefs keep the familiar patches at the center of its choices, while the rest of the arena has little pull. A hypercanalized person may be in a similar position: seeing the world through a narrow ``strip map'', with few options or ideas in view and little means of coping when the environment changes unexpectedly. Decanalization relaxes precision, so that new observations count for more. For such a person, the field of experience might then take in more options---more possibilities to draw on in what William James called the ``fringe'' of awareness \citep{james_principles_1890, mangan_taking_1993}.

\subsection{Limitations}

The two acute changes we used as decanalizing interventions---relaxing the precision of the cognitive map and raising the temperature of the option policy---are modeling decisions. They are motivated by the REBUS and CANAL frameworks, but these frameworks suggest other changes too, such as acutely altering the policy's weights or its learning rate. Temporary decanalization could be formalized in other ways. Our results therefore show only what follows from our choices and how we implemented them. For example, our policy's choices are coarse: every half second it picks one of four hand-coded options, and the steering that carries each out is fixed. The cognitive map is also minimal. Position and geometry are given and only the contents of cells are learned, so the agent lacks the learned spatial and relational structure of cognitive maps in Tolman's or Behrens's sense \citep{tolman_cognitive_1948, behrens_cognitive_2018}. Since its weights are fixed, the policy does not learn during or after the intervention. The lasting effect of decanalization therefore runs entirely through the state it leaves behind: the agent's beliefs, its position and homeostatic state, and the world wherever the agent's behavior has altered it.

Our implementation of hypercanalization required that the world change in ways the policy had not been trained on. This follows from the theory, but it means the measured effect size likely depends on the size of the mismatch between the stable world, used for training, and the changing world, used for evaluation. We tested only the two extremes: the stable-trained policies of our main comparison, and a policy trained in the changing world itself (one HRL training seed), which was rarely trapped and which decanalization did not help (Appendix Table~\ref{tab:main}). The changing world also supplies a better alternative; without one, escaping a failing patch is less useful. The size of the benefit depends, too, on the length of the recanalization phase. Many CCC agents reached the new patches late or not at all: more than a third of HRL CCC agents never harvested from them, and those that did first harvested from them at a median of 495\,s, nearly a third of the way through the recanalization phase. With a longer phase, more of them would likely find the new patches and recover, and the difference in MAE would shrink. The model also attaches no cost to extreme divergence beyond the reward itself: $h$ is unbounded and an agent cannot die, whereas in real organisms starvation and overeating carry both acute and long-term risks.

Our main measure, MAE, counts surplus and deficit alike. However, the NF objective penalizes only deficit, and decanalized NF agents ended far above the setpoint. In this sense, MAE judges NF agents against a goal they were not trained for. This is partly the point of the comparison, since HRL is rewarded for what MAE measures, but it means the difference between HRL and NF reflects their objectives as well as their response to decanalization. 

We also selected each agent by how well it regulated in the stable training world, so selection favored agents with policies that regulate well when nothing changes. This was intentional, but it bears emphasizing. The policy trained in the changing world avoided being trapped, but it had been trained on the very change it was evaluated on. Whether training in changing worlds also prepares agents for changes they have not seen is a separate---but interesting---question. In reinforcement learning, training on more varied environments improves generalization to new ones \citep{cobbe_quantifying_2019}, and human development may show a similar pattern. People who have faced some adversity cope better with later stressors than those who have faced none or a great deal \citep{seery_whatever_2010}. Highly unpredictable environments, in turn, favor strategies that pay off quickly over ones that depend on a stable future \citep{ellis_fundamental_2009}, although they can also improve some forms of flexibility \citep{mittal_cognitive_2015}.

The policies are also unlikely to be optimal. We trained them with off-the-shelf PPO and only limited tuning, and we deliberately limited the agent's perception to a $90^\circ$ field reaching 10 tiles ahead, since real agents never observe their world fully; partial observation makes a task harder to learn well. The policy trained in the changing world regulated well (MAE 12.5), so the task can be learned, but we do not know how far any of the policies falls short of optimal. Maximizing performance was not our aim: in domains such as Atari games and language modeling, strong performance has required very large amounts of compute and expert engineering \citep{mnih_human-level_2015, badia_agent57_2020, hoffmann_training_2022}. Our comparisons are between conditions run with the same policies, so they depend less on how good the policies are than absolute performance would. Still, a better policy might be trapped less often, and so gain less from decanalization.

Our agents managed a single setpoint. Living organisms coordinate many setpoints, allowing some to stray far from the ideal range so that others may be satisfied. Setpoints themselves change over time, often in anticipation of demand, as in allostasis \citep{sterling_allostasis_1988}. 

We also did not model integration---the work done after an acute experience to process and carry its effects into daily life. Psychedelic-assisted therapy pairs acute alterations in experience with active integration practices \citep{bathje_psychedelic_2022}, and patients receive support before, during, and after treatment. In our model, the recanalization phase simply returns the agent to its baseline settings. 

\subsection{Future directions}

The present study applies a single intervention to fixed-weight agents. A natural next step is to allow learning during and after the intervention. Accounts of critical-period plasticity propose that psychedelics can reopen critical periods \citep{dolen_emerging_2026}. In mice, psychedelics temporarily reopen a critical period for social reward learning, and the length of the reopened window tracks how long each drug's acute subjective effects last in humans \citep{nardou_psychedelics_2023}. In humans, LSD increased learning rates during the acute state \citep{kanen_effect_2023}, and in a small study of healthy volunteers, psilocybin reduced confidence in negative self-beliefs four weeks later \citep{zeifman_rebus_2025}. With learning in place, repeated cycles of decanalization and recanalization could show which schedules merely restore regulation and which produce lasting improvement. We expect a long-term Goldilocks zone, much like the intermediate temperature boost that worked best for HRL agents in a single intervention. With too little decanalization, the agent stays in its rut. With too much, it is constantly destabilized and never has time to consolidate what it finds, so repeated decanalization would eventually degrade performance and could prevent useful canalization. Somewhere in between, periodic decanalization---followed by enough recanalization to learn from it---should produce the best long-term regulation, much as annealing depends on its cooling schedule \citep{kirkpatrick_optimization_1983, hajek_cooling_1988}. 

The conditions after the intervention are also important \citep{carhart-harris_context_2018, dolen_emerging_2026}. To model these, we could make new patches available during recanalization that vary in quality, density, and distance. The same framework could test robustness to new disruptions, a computational analogue of adversity or trauma: shift or deplete the resources the agent relies on, or move its setpoint, and measure how quickly different kinds of agents recover. Agents could also manage several homeostatic variables at once, which would bring more complex trade-offs and a richer set of behaviors. The experiment could also be run with an active inference agent \citep{pezzulo_active_2015}, in which both components of the intervention would be precisions in a single generative model. More broadly, our implementation could be extended to vary the timing, intensity, and frequency of interventions and the conditions that follow them, tracking both short- and long-term outcomes.

Our results, and the modeling decisions behind them, bear on a broader debate about dosing and the shape of therapeutic change. One view treats change as incremental: give a small intervention, observe its effects, and raise or lower the dose accordingly. This logic is familiar from dose titration in pharmacotherapy and from graded, step-by-step exposure in behavioral and cognitive-behavioral therapy. It may also explain the continued appeal of psychedelic microdosing, even though placebo-controlled studies have found little benefit beyond placebo \citep{szigeti_microdosing_2021, cavanna_microdosing_2022, marschall_psilocybin_2022}. 

An alternative model, from integrative psychotherapy and synergetics \citep{schiepek_self-organization_2014, schiepek_psychotherapy_2017}, brings criticality into psychological transformation. In this model, change is not smooth: a system near instability reorganizes during one or a few highly transformative episodes, such as those characterized as pivotal mental states \citep{brouwer_pivotal_2021}. Philosophers have similarly described transformative experiences, which change what a person knows and cares about \citep{paul_transformative_2014}. Discontinuous change appears even within cognitive-behavioral therapy, where some patients show large, sudden gains between sessions \citep{tang_sudden_1999, hayes_change_2007}. Our model could compare these possibilities directly. For example, we could give agents either one large perturbation or many small ones of matched total size, evaluating which produces more durable improvements in regulation. The present results are partly consistent with the pivotal view: for HRL agents, a single intervention improved later regulation, and a full dose helped more than a half dose. However, the largest temperature boost helped less than a moderate one, suggesting that---at least for this component---the best intervention lies between the extremes. 

\section{Conclusions}

Research on canalization and decanalization often moves between clinical, dynamical, and mechanistic vocabularies without making computational assumptions explicit. Our aim was to formalize one such set of assumptions in a simple model that could be inspected, perturbed, and compared across conditions.

An agent that learns confident beliefs about a stable world often becomes trapped when that world changes. Temporarily relaxing the precision of those beliefs and making its choices more random helps it find new resources sooner and regulate better in the immediate aftermath. The benefit is much larger for an agent that values staying near its setpoint (HRL) than for an agent whose reward depends only on its deficit (NF). This suggests that decanalization may help people manage their needs, although the effect depends on context: in our model, it required that the world had changed and that a better alternative was available. It should help most when entrenched beliefs keep someone from taking advantage of new, rewarding possibilities.

Like any scientific model, ours is a simplified approximation of a much more complex reality. We kept it small for tractability, and in doing so likely omitted emergent and higher-level features that are central to behavioral transformation. The technical and parameter choices described in the limitations may also have shaped the results. These are standard trade-offs in scientific modeling, and the price of studying behavioral entrenchment with a concrete model. The model should therefore be read as a formal demonstration of one candidate mechanism for behavioral change in the face of deeply entrenched beliefs---we nevertheless hope it provides a useful starting point for further investigation.
