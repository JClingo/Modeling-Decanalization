We thank both reviewers for helpful comments. They led to fairly substantial changes both to the model and the paper. The simulation is now a two-dimensional world in pure Python, and everything can be regenerated from the public repository. Canalization is now located in learning, as Reviewer 1 recommended.

Page and line numbers below refer to the revised manuscript, with lines numbered on each page. Quoted passages are new or rewritten text.

REVIEWER 1

Comment: My biggest issue with the paper as it currently exists is that the choice of reward function is not well justified within the paper itself. [...] My recommendation would be to focus on canalization that is connected to the intrinsic dynamics of learning itself. If, however, the focus remains on the design of the reward function, then that choice should be thoroughly justified and defended within the paper. It may also be valuable to design a non-canalization-inducing reward function which can be compared against the canalizing one.

Response: We took this recommendation. Canalization now arises from learning, not the reward function. The "healthy" comparison is provided by agents with low-precision beliefs, which are trapped much less often.

- Methods, p. 7, ll. 4-7: "In this model, canalization corresponds to the precision of the agent's expectations in its cognitive map: having specific expectations about where food is corresponds to being canalized."
- Methods, p. 14, ll. 10-11: "Finally, to check whether precise beliefs are what trap the agent, we ran each policy with beliefs that never become precise and with no intervention."
- Results, p. 16, l. 32 to p. 17, l. 2: "Compared with the low-precision control—which keeps precision low for the whole trial—agents with precise beliefs were trapped more often in the recanalization phase, by 24 percentage points for HRL and 33 for NF."

Comment: Page 3 (Footnote): Deep CANALs is the Juliani et al. (2024) model, while CANAL is the Carhart-Harris et al. (2023) model.

Response: Corrected in the main text, and the footnote was removed.

Response: The Introduction now ends with two explicit hypotheses and alludes to our findings.

Second, in the same worlds and with the same intervention, an HRL agent will benefit more than an NF agent. Both hypotheses held in our simulations. Decanalization helped agents find new resources sooner and regulate better afterward, and HRL agents benefited far more than NF agents, which escaped their failing patches but consistently overate."

Comment: Page 7: I am not clear on the action space available to the agent.

The four options are to seek the remembered cell with the greatest believed worth, discounted by distance; explore toward the nearest cell never seen; cruise along the cheapest (most worn) direction; or avoid visible food."

Comment: A related question is: "why did this need to be simulated at all?"

Response: We think the paper as a whole now makes the answer to this more clear. The Introduction in particular now states the rationale: these concepts have mostly been used metaphorically, and a working model makes their assumptions explicit and testable. Several results were not obvious in advance, and our particular set of modeling choices and sensitivity analysis of some of these demonstrates what can happen when some of the assumptions of the field are included/excluded. These broader ideas should now be more clear in the Discussion and Future directions sections.

- Introduction, p. 3, ll. 5-9: "For the most part, these concepts have remained metaphorical. Existing conceptualizations are often suggestive, but they rarely model their assumptions directly. [...] To address this gap, we developed a simulation that translates some of these assumptions into observable behavior."
- Conclusions, p. 29, ll. 18-22: "Research on canalization and decanalization often moves between clinical, dynamical, and mechanistic vocabularies without making computational assumptions explicit. Our aim was to formalize one such set of assumptions in a simple model that could be inspected, perturbed, and compared across conditions."

REVIEWER 2

Comment: "Canalization and plasticity in psychopathology" is a highly problematic paper [...] it would be more accurate (even if less satisfying/aggrandizing) to refer to this as the "second principal component" of psychopathology.

Response: We now present Carhart-Harris et al.'s claim as a proposal and contrast it with cybernetic accounts, on which the general factor corresponds to low Stability (Discussion). We also note that a single-agent model like ours cannot settle which dimension is general.

- Discussion, p. 23, ll. 23-36: "Carhart-Harris et al. (2023) propose that canalization, an entrenched rigidity of beliefs and behavior, is the general factor of psychopathology. Cybernetic accounts of personality, which place goal regulation at the center of individual differences, read the general factor differently. [...] A model like ours with a single kind of agent cannot settle which dimension is general, since that is a question about how individual humans differ."

Comment: In discussing altered beliefs under psychedelics, it could be worth mentioning this work which suggests we ought to expect combinations of both directly and indirectly relaxed and strengthened beliefs (ALBUS).

Response: Added to the Discussion.

- Discussion, p. 22, ll. 36-38: "Psychedelic effects may involve both relaxed and strengthened beliefs, depending on representational level, dose, and context (Safron et al., 2025); our intervention only relaxes."

Comment: Another question I had in reading this manuscript was the extent to which these findings could be used to understand the natural/normative functioning of psychedelic-like transformative experiences as described in the excellent work of Ari Brouwer.

Response: Added to the Discussion.

- Discussion, p. 24, ll. 7-16: "Decanalization need not come from outside. Brouwer & Carhart-Harris (2021) propose that pivotal mental states—brief periods of heightened plasticity that can arise naturally under stress—help people leave patterns that no longer serve them. The psychedelic experience has been described as following the narrative arc of the Hero's Journey, with a departure, initiation, and return (Brouwer et al., 2024). Our three phases have a similar shape. Read this way, our implementation suggests one reason such pivotal states could be adaptive: a brief loosening, coming when the world has changed, shortens the time spent far from the setpoint, avoiding the homeostatic consequences of extreme divergence."
